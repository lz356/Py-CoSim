## Set-up

All experiments use EnergyPlus 22.2 [@crawley2001] and the small office reference building of the U.S. Department of Energy (511 m^2^, five conditioned zones, packaged single-zone air conditioners with direct-expansion cooling) [@deru2011] with the weather file for Tucson, Arizona, from the third typical meteorological year data set (TMY3) [@wilcox2008]. Daylight-saving time is removed from the model, as Arizona does not observe it. Unless stated otherwise, the period is the week from Monday 17 July to Sunday 23 July and the exchange step is 15 min. The computer is a laptop with an Intel Core i7-1185G7 processor (4 cores, 8 threads) and 16 GB of memory, running Windows 11 and Python 3.12. Every number in this section is produced by scripts in the repository, which also contains the raw results.

## Verification against standalone EnergyPlus {#sec:verification}

The EnergyPlus adapter must not change what EnergyPlus computes. Three checks over the test week (672 steps) confirm this. (V1) The model run inside Py-CoSim with sensors only and the same model run from the EnergyPlus command line give identical outputs: the maximum absolute difference over seven time series (five zone temperatures, the outdoor temperature and the facility electricity) is zero. (V2) The value Py-CoSim publishes at master time $t$ equals the value EnergyPlus reports with time stamp $t$ (maximum difference zero). (V3) A Python controller that writes a pre-cooling profile to the cooling set-point through an actuator gives results identical to the same profile hard-coded as an EnergyPlus schedule (maximum difference zero), while the profile changes zone temperatures by up to 2.5 K relative to the original schedule. The co-simulation is therefore exact with respect to EnergyPlus itself, and any difference in the following experiments comes from the exchange of data between components.

## Alignment across step sizes {#sec:e2a}

The first experiment isolates the exchange of interval data, using a signal whose exact integral is known. A producer publishes the exact mean, over each of its steps, of an analytic electric load: 50 kW plus a daily sinusoid of 30 kW amplitude and a 3-h oscillation of 12 kW. Its messages are of kind `average` over the step that has just ended, like an EnergyPlus meter. A tariff publishes either a time-of-use (TOU) price (0.30 $/kWh on weekdays from 16:00 to 21:00, 0.10 $/kWh otherwise) or a real-time price that changes every 5 min. A grid meter integrates energy and cost at its own step. Producer and meter steps take all combinations of 1, 5, 15, 30 and 60 min over three days. With naive coupling, the meter multiplies the latest power by the latest price. With aligned coupling, it reads both over its step that has just ended. The exact energy and cost over the meter's windows are integrated at 1-s resolution.

The cost errors are shown in @fig:e2a. With naive coupling, cost errors reach 6.0 % for the TOU price and 4.1 % for the real-time price, and energy errors reach @@E2A_NAIVE_ENERGY@@ %, even when producer and meter run at the same step (1.4 % at 15 min, 6.0 % at 60 min for TOU). The error at equal steps comes entirely from charging the energy of one interval at the price of the next. With aligned coupling, whenever the meter's step is a multiple of the producer's step, energy and TOU cost are exact to within @@E2A_ALIGNED_TOU@@ % (rounding). For the real-time price, a residual of at most 0.011 % remains when the meter is slower than the price signal, because the mean of a product is not the product of the means within the meter's step. That residual is a property of the meter's resolution, not of the exchange. When the meter runs faster than the producer (the upper-left triangles in @fig:e2a), no message covers the current producer interval yet. TIDE then holds the last complete interval and reports the missing coverage in the run's alignment table, and errors of up to 5.9 % remain in both modes.

## Coupling error with EnergyPlus {#sec:e2b}

The second coupling experiment uses the office with a 30 kW PV array, a 100 kWh battery, the TOU tariff and a grid meter. To isolate numerical effects, all control actions are schedules: pre-cooling to 22 °C from 13:00 to 16:00 and a set-up to 26.7 °C from 16:00 to 21:00 on weekdays, and a battery that charges at 20 kW from 10:00 to 14:00 and discharges at 25 kW from 16:00 to 20:00 on weekdays. The physical system is then the same whatever the exchange step. A reference run exchanges data every minute with EnergyPlus at 60 steps per hour. Each exchange step of 5, 15, 30 and 60 min is then run in three ways: naive coupling, in which a helper component adds the latest building, PV and battery powers and the meter multiplies the result by the latest price (the conventional wiring); aligned coupling, in which the meter subscribes to the three flows and the price with `read="past"`; and aligned coupling in which EnergyPlus keeps a 1-min internal step and exchanges every 5 to 60 min.

![Cost error of a grid meter for every combination of producer and meter step: (a, b) naive exchange of the latest values; (c, d) aligned exchange with `read="past"`; (a, c) time-of-use price; (b, d) real-time price changing every 5 min. Values below 0.001 % are shown as "<0.001".](../figures/fig05_alignment_synthetic.png){#fig:e2a}

A first attempt revealed a pitfall that has nothing to do with co-simulation. Standalone EnergyPlus runs of the same model, made from the command line, used 11 % to 14 % less electricity with 1 to 12 steps per hour than with 60 steps per hour (@fig:e2b(d)). The cause is autosizing. The model's sizing parameters average design loads over six time steps, which is 90 min at the model's original 4 steps per hour but only 6 min at 60 steps per hour; the short window catches the morning cool-down peak and sizes the cooling coils and fans 65 % larger (54.5 kW instead of 33.1 kW of total cooling capacity). With the averaging window fixed at 90 min for every time step, the standalone results differ by less than 0.7 %. All co-simulations below use the fixed window, so that every variant simulates the same equipment.

The errors relative to the reference (1371 kWh grid import, 399 kWh export and 154.76 $ energy cost for the week) are shown in @fig:e2b. With naive coupling, the cost error grows from 1.1 % at a 5-min step to 4.8 % at 60 min, and the export error reaches 8.2 %, because the battery power that applies to the coming hour is added to the building power of the past hour and both are priced at the coming hour's price. Aligned coupling at the same exchange steps reduces the cost error to 0.8–1.3 % and the export error to at most 0.5 %. When EnergyPlus also keeps its 1-min internal step, the building's electricity equals the reference exactly at every exchange step, and the remaining grid errors (at most 1.7 % for cost and 1.0 % for import, at 60 min) come from the meter itself: a meter with an hourly step nets imports against exports within the hour, and the PV output, an instantaneous signal, is integrated by trapezoids over the exchange step. These are properties of the chosen meter resolution, not of the exchange; with an hourly exchange, peak demand over 15 min cannot be resolved either (the 60-min variants underestimate it by about 6 %). Running EnergyPlus with a 1-min internal step costs about 40–60 s per simulated week, against 6–27 s at the exchange step, almost all of it inside EnergyPlus.

## Reproducibility with a non-deterministic supervisor {#sec:e3}

The office is now supervised by an LLM supervisor component that decides the cooling set-point every hour from the core-zone temperature, the outdoor temperature and the price. Its back end is a stochastic stand-in for a hosted LLM: it draws a random offset (standard deviation 1 K, rounded to 0.5 K) around a rule-based answer, with an unseeded random generator, so each run differs as a sampled LLM would. The stand-in is not a language model; it imitates the property that matters here.

![Coupling error of the EnergyPlus, PV, battery, tariff and meter co-simulation relative to a 1-min reference, for exchange steps of 5 to 60 min: (a) energy cost, (b) grid import, (c) grid export, each with naive coupling, aligned coupling, and aligned coupling with a 1-min EnergyPlus step inside a coarser exchange step; (d) facility electricity of standalone EnergyPlus runs with 1 to 60 time steps per hour, with the model's sizing window counted in time steps and fixed at 90 min.](../figures/fig06_coupling_energyplus.png){#fig:e2b}

Five live runs differ substantially (@fig:e3a). The hourly set-points of runs 1 and 2 differ by up to 4 K, the core-zone temperature by up to 4.0 K and the facility power by up to 6.2 kW at individual steps. The weekly energy varies between runs by 11.8 kWh (standard deviation 5.0 kWh, 0.3 %) and the cost by 1.87 $. Run 1 recorded the 672 messages of the supervisor. Replaying them into a new run, with the supervisor replaced by its recording, reproduced run 1 exactly: the maximum absolute difference over all physical outputs (zone temperatures, power, set-points, energy and cost) was zero.

Replay also enables controlled comparisons. Suppose the question is how much a 30 % reduction of lighting power saves when the building is operated by the same stochastic supervisor. Comparing each live base run with a live run of the retrofitted building mixes the retrofit effect with fresh random decisions; the five estimates range from 8.0 % to 9.0 % (8.57 ± 0.38 %). Replaying each base run's decisions into the retrofitted building holds the decisions fixed, as in the method of common random numbers. The five estimates then agree within 0.04 percentage points (8.56 ± 0.016 %), a 24-fold reduction of the spread (@fig:e3a(b)). The replayed comparison answers a narrower question, the effect of the retrofit under identical decisions rather than under decisions that react to the retrofitted building, and the two should be reported for what they are.

## Slow components {#sec:e4}

To study latency, $N$ offices ($N$ = 1, 2, 4, 8) are each supervised hourly by their own LLM supervisor over two days (48 decisions per building). The stand-in back end now answers deterministically but waits for a log-normally distributed time (median 0.5 s, shape parameter 0.5) before answering, imitating the latency of a hosted model; a call took 0.52 s on average. In the blocking configuration, the master waits for every answer, and a decision applies at the time step it was requested for. In the asynchronous configuration, each supervisor is declared asynchronous with a decision delay of 15 min, so its call runs in a background thread and its answer applies one step later in simulation time.

![Record and replay with a stochastic supervisor (stand-in for an LLM service): (a) cooling set-points chosen on 18 July in five live runs and in the replay of run 1, which coincides with run 1; (b) savings of a lighting retrofit estimated from pairs of live runs and from live runs paired with replays of their decisions in the retrofitted building.](../figures/fig07_replay.png){#fig:e3a}

The wall-clock times are shown in @fig:e4. Blocking calls add up: 34, 63, 124 and 238 s for 1, 2, 4 and 8 buildings, almost exactly the sum of all call latencies plus the simulation. With asynchronous delivery, the calls of all buildings overlap with each other and with EnergyPlus, and each decision round costs roughly the latency of the slowest call: 34, 49, 57 and 82 s, a speed-up of 1.0, 1.3, 2.2 and 2.9. With one building there is nothing to overlap, because an EnergyPlus step takes far less time than a call. Each asynchronous run was repeated with new random latencies, and for every $N$ the two runs were identical (maximum absolute difference zero), as intended: what happens in the co-simulation depends on the declared decision delay, not on how long the calls took. The delay itself is part of the model. Applying the same decisions 15 min later changed the two-day electricity use by 0.32 %. Since real decisions also arrive late, the asynchronous configuration is arguably the more realistic one. Calls that miss a wall-clock deadline keep the previous decision and are flagged `late`; this path is covered by the test suite rather than by this experiment.

## Overhead {#sec:e6}

The features of TIDE cost little next to a building model. Publishing a message and reading the latest value takes 4.3 µs; reading a window that spans 15 messages takes 58 µs, as the reconstruction runs in pure Python. In a co-simulation without EnergyPlus that stresses the hub, 50 Python producers publish 15-min averages and an hourly meter subscribes to all of them with one pattern (30 days, 2880 steps). The master then needs 34 µs per component and step with latest reads and 39 µs with aligned reads. Recording all 144,720 messages raises this to 61 µs, mostly for building the message table at the end of the run, and the provenance trace adds 3 µs more. @@SCALING@@

## Comparison with HELICS {#sec:e7}

HELICS is the closest established tool: a message-based co-simulation framework for energy systems [@hardy2024]. The office scenario with the pre-cooling schedule, the TOU tariff and a meter (15-min exchange, one week, 90-min sizing window) was implemented in HELICS 3.6.1 with a broker and three federates in separate processes: an EnergyPlus building federate written on the EnergyPlus Python API, a controller and tariff federate, and a meter federate. HELICS ships no EnergyPlus federate, so the building federate had to be written, including its own time stamps. With the `wait_for_current_time_update` option on the building and meter federates, HELICS reproduced the Py-CoSim run with latest-value reads bit for bit over all 672 steps (power, set-point and price identical; cost difference 10^−14^ $). Without that option, every link lagged by one period and the set-point took effect 15 min late.

Both tools, exchanging latest values, charged the week's energy 1.10 % (2.65 $) more than the aligned Py-CoSim meter, all of it in the ten intervals at the tariff boundaries (16:00 and 21:00 on weekdays). The aligned result can also be obtained in HELICS, but only by coding the pairing of each past-interval power with the price that applied to it into the meter federate, because HELICS values carry no interval meaning; Py-CoSim's library meter does it from the declared time meanings. Fed with a 1-min EnergyPlus run, the 15-min aligned meter matched the 1-min reference to within 10^−13^, while latest-value pairing was 1.09 % high. The scenario took 17 lines of Python with Py-CoSim's library components and 131 lines of Python plus 46 lines of JSON configuration with HELICS, most of them for the EnergyPlus federate. Run times were comparable (12–15 s for the simulated week, including process start-up). This comparison reflects what a typical user would write for this scenario; HELICS's strengths in distributed, multi-language and very large co-simulations, iteration and unit conversion are outside its scope (@tbl:features).

## Case study: a grid-interactive community {#sec:case}

The last experiment shows what alignment changes in a typical study. Ten small offices (floor areas varied by ±10 %), each a separate EnergyPlus instance in its own worker process, share a 250 kW PV array, a 500 kWh battery and one grid connection under a TOU tariff (0.30 $/kWh from 16:00 to 21:00 on weekdays, 0.08 $/kWh otherwise). Four strategies are compared over the test week: a baseline, pre-cooling of all offices before the price peak, a battery that charges from PV surplus, discharges on peak and shaves the import above 120 kW, and both together (coordinated). Every strategy is run twice. In the naive version, a helper component adds the latest building, PV and battery powers and the meter multiplies the sum by the latest price, as is common practice. In the aligned version, the meter subscribes to all buildings with the pattern `bldg*.P_facility` and to the PV and battery outputs, and reads all of them and the price over the 15 min that just ended. The coordinated co-simulation has 18 components (10 of them EnergyPlus) and runs in about 30 s.

The coupling changes the conclusions about the battery (@fig:case). With naive coupling, the coordinated strategy reduces the energy cost by 32.7 % and the on-peak import by 83.3 %, and its peak import equals the battery's import limit of 120 kW exactly. With aligned coupling, the reductions are 30.1 % and 79.5 %, and the peak import is 135 kW. The naive meter adds the battery power that the controller chose for the coming 15 min to the net load of the 15 min that just ended, which is the very load the controller reacted to; it therefore credits the controller with perfect foresight. The aligned meter adds the battery power to the interval in which it actually flowed, exposing the controller's one-step lag. For the pre-cooling strategy, which has no battery, the two versions differ by less than 1 percentage point in cost savings. Any study that exchanges latest values would overstate the benefits of such battery strategies in the same way.
